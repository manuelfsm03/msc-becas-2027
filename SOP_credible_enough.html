1 Hook + tesis 101 w · presup. 120–150

At seventeen I spent twenty minutes convincing my secondary school that climate change was a fabrication. It was a TED-Ed talk. The charts looked real, the sources did not exist, and halfway through I took the whole thing apart in front of the audience, showing them line by line how each piece of evidence had been manufactured and how quickly they had accepted it.

The subject was never the climate. It was how little it costs to make something look true.

That question has organised my academic life since. At what point does a claim become credible enough to act on?

2 La pregunta hecha economía 168 w · presup. 150–180

I did not have a discipline for it until a course on the epistemology of economics with Diego Weisman. Philosophy of science is what made economics matter to me. It was there that I saw what an economic claim has to do to earn belief, and there that I first read Duflo, and Angrist and Pischke. A course in development economics with Juan Pablo González kept it going.

The most serious answer I found was in applied econometrics, in what Angrist and Pischke called the credibility revolution: the turn towards research designs that make a causal claim answerable instead of assumed. To me it reads as epistemology carried out with data. Identification strategies are how the discipline made itself accountable to the problem of knowing, and development economics is where that has been pushed hardest, because the cost of being wrong is counted in people. Policy built on correlation is policy built on manufactured evidence. It is the same failure I staged at seventeen, with the stakes moved.

3 Evidencia I · investigación 247 w · presup. 200–230

At the IIEP I joined MESi, the applied modelling group at the University of Buenos Aires, and co-authored a study on biomass energy in the Argentine Mesopotamia, the provinces that hold most of the country's forest-industrial residue and almost none of its renewable-energy policy attention. It was my first time working with economists of that standing, and it raised the bar I hold my own work to. We built a regional input–output model and used it to weigh biomass expansion against the thermoelectric generation it would displace. Biomass came out ahead on every dimension we could measure: output, employment, emissions.

What stayed with me was not the result. It was the paragraph where we wrote down what the model could not tell anyone. It could show how a shock travelled through a structure we had specified in advance. It could not show that any intervention had caused anything, because that was settled in the calibration before the model ever ran. A policy reader would not have known the difference unless we said so.

So we said so. A model like that one can only hand back the structure you gave it: its coefficients are the assumptions, and the result is their arithmetic. It can be internally wrong, but it cannot be surprised. I want to be trained in the methods that can be, where the variation comes from the world rather than from me, and where a finding is allowed to overturn the belief that prompted it.

4 Evidencia II · aplicada 148 w · presup. 130–160

At Bunge my work sits between the data and the people who trade on it, which mostly means testing what the desk already believes. The most useful thing I have produced there was a negative result. Traders held that the corn–soybean relative price drove the farmer's decision of which crop to sell. It is a good story. It has a mechanism, and everyone on the floor could recite it. I tested it NEED · método. It was not a driver.

Telling a trading floor that its working rule is not in the data is a harder conversation than any I have had in a classroom. It is also the one I most want to be equipped for. A market-share series I started calculating on my own now runs as a daily report to the CEO for the Southern Cone, and commercial teams across the region set their decisions by it. 5HSG St. Gallen184 w · presup. 180–220
MEcon · Public Policymétodo causal y su escala macro, bajo un mismo techoprofes verificados: Lechner ✓ · Koeniger ✓ (10 sep)

St. Gallen is the one place I have found where both halves of this question sit in the same building. At SEW-HSG, Michael Lechner's group develops causal machine learning for the evaluation of public programmes, and releases the tools, such as the mcf package, that let the method travel beyond the paper. Winfried Koeniger's work on how monetary and fiscal policy transmit, identified with high-frequency transaction data, takes that discipline to the level of the macroeconomy. That is exactly the step my own work stopped short of.

The specialisation in Public Policy would let me build the MEcon around that pairing: econometrics first, then policy analysis, with the GSERM course in Causal Inference as the bridge. I am also drawn to a department that treats advising government as part of its work rather than an afterthought, because that is the setting I am training for.

I would come to St. Gallen with a specific question: which of the policy claims I hear repeated in Buenos Aires would survive a design that could prove them wrong. I would like to leave able to answer it.

5Warwick173 w · presup. 180–220
MSc Economicscliometría: la identificación en el pasadoprofes verificados: Ricco ✓ · Fenske ✓ · Gupta ✓ (25 sep)

Warwick answers the question with history. The Global Economic History programme at CAGE, where James Fenske and Bishnupriya Gupta work, does for the past what I want to learn to do for the present: it finds the accidents of borders, institutions and colonial rule that let a causal claim be tested rather than asserted. Their recent work on colonial persistence is the same genre as the Mississippi study that convinced me macroeconomic questions can be identified at all.

The other half is Giovanni Ricco's work on monetary policy shocks. His argument, that the instruments macroeconomists usually rely on mix the true shock with the central bank's information about the economy, is the Sims objection taken seriously and answered with better identification. It is the closest thing I have found to the problem I want to work on at the Central Bank.

CAGE's Responsive Public Policy programme closes the loop from evidence to decision. Warwick is where I would learn both to find the variation and to be honest about what it can carry.

5Bristol168 w · presup. 180–220
MSc Economicstreatment effects y policy learningprofes verificados: Han ✓ (25 sep) · Zylberberg ⚠️

Bristol has turned my question into an institution. The Centre for Evidence-based Public Services exists to make public decisions answerable to evidence, and the School's econometrics group lists causal inference, treatment effects and policy learning as a declared area. No other department I considered describes its purpose in those terms.

Sukjin Han's research is the most direct version of what I want to learn. His work on identifying treatment effects for policy evaluation, and more recently on policy learning that accounts for who gains and who loses, addresses exactly what the model in my biomass study could not: whether an intervention changed anything, and for whom. Yanos Zylberberg's work on spatial and development macroeconomics shows how those designs scale up to questions about regions and growth, which is where the Argentine Mesopotamia sits.

I want the MSc to give me the formal training to read, and eventually produce, work of that kind. Bristol is the programme where the econometrics and the policy purpose are taught as one subject.

5Southampton170 w · presup. 180–220
MSc Economicsseries de tiempo: la identificación propia de la macroprofes verificados: Pitarakis ✓ · Magdalinos ✓ · Olmo ✓ (25 sep)

Sims's objection was never that macroeconomics cannot identify anything. It was that the toolkit of applied microeconomics does not transfer. Macroeconomics built its own tradition of identification in time series, starting with the vector autoregressions Sims himself introduced, and Southampton is one of the places where that tradition is strongest.

The econometrics group describes its work as spanning time-series methods, financial econometrics and machine-learning methods applied to problems in causal inference. Anastasios Magdalinos's work on inference in cointegrated and mildly explosive systems, and Jean-Yves Pitarakis's work on predictive regressions, are the kind of training a macroeconomic question actually demands: knowing when a relationship in the data is a finding and when it is an artefact of persistence. José Olmo's work in financial econometrics and monetary economics connects those methods to the central bank I hope to work for.

The other programmes I am applying to would teach me the micro answer to the credibility problem. Southampton would teach me the macro one, which is the one Argentina's policy questions need.

5Glasgow151 w · presup. 180–220
MSc Economicshistoria económica cuantitativaprofes verificados: Trew ✓ · Montoya-Blandón ✓ (25 sep)

Glasgow's attraction for me is a department where quantitative history and careful econometrics are practised side by side. Alex Trew's work on infrastructure and the spatial take-off of the Industrial Revolution, and his recent finding that historical air pollution still shapes how neighbourhoods sort today, is identification by long memory: the past as a source of variation the present cannot manufacture.

Santiago Montoya-Blandón's teaching and research in applied and Bayesian econometrics would give me the other half, the formal discipline to state what a given design can and cannot support. His background in Latin America also makes him a natural reader of the questions I would bring from Argentina.

The Applied Economics cluster would be my home in the programme. I want to leave Glasgow able to ask of any regional policy in Argentina what Trew asks of the Industrial Revolution: what would have happened otherwise, and how we can know.

5Birmingham182 w · presup. 180–220
MSc Economicspolítica fiscal: la otra mitad de la macroprofes verificados: Rossi ✓ · Banerjee ✓ (25 sep)

Most of the debate about whether macroeconomics can be identified is about monetary policy. In Argentina the harder question is usually fiscal: what a tax change or a spending rule actually does to output and prices, as opposed to what the budget speech says it will do. Birmingham is where I would learn to answer it.

Raffaele Rossi's work sits exactly on that line. His research with Nguyen and Onnis on the macroeconomic effects of income and consumption tax changes separates the effect of the policy from the circumstances that prompted it, and his work on fiscal rules and on monetary and fiscal interaction asks the design questions a Ministry of Economy faces. I want the MSc to give me the tools to take a fiscal claim apart the way I learned to take apart a trading-floor rule, and Birmingham is the programme where fiscal policy is treated as an empirical question rather than only a theoretical one.

6 Endgame 189 w · presup. 150–180

I want to work on macroeconomic policy in Argentina, at the Ministry of Economy or the Central Bank. The objection to that comes from serious people. Replying to Angrist and Pischke in 2010, Christopher Sims held that natural experiments are rhetorical devices rather than experiments, and that what the credibility revolution claims about macroeconomics is mainly nonsense.

I take it seriously. I also think the cliometric tradition has been quietly answering it for decades. The Federal Reserve Act split Mississippi between two districts that then ran opposite doctrines through the Depression, and the arbitrary border it left behind gave Richardson and Troost as clean a test of central bank intervention as macroeconomics is likely to get. Banks on the activist side survived at higher rates, and recovery came sooner. NEED · un ejemplo argentino equivalente. Si no aparece uno bueno, se corta la oración

The part I already know I love is the last step. Teaching at the FCE-UBA is where I learned that an argument is only finished when someone who has to decide something can use it. At seventeen I showed a room how cheaply I could make them believe something. I would like to spend a career on the opposite problem.
